### The Regulatory, Technical, and Geopolitical Dynamics Shaping Pakistan’s AI future.

The emergence of Pakistan as a significant node in the global digital economy is the result of a deliberate, multi-decadal institutional pivot aimed at transforming the country from a provider of low-cost software services to a regional epicenter for compute-intensive infrastructure. This transition is underpinned by a sophisticated regulatory framework, a radical re-engineering of energy utilization strategies, and the leveraging of unique geographic advantages that connect the terrestrial networks of East Asia with the submarine cables of the Indian Ocean. The contemporary landscape, characterized by the 2025-26 fiscal reforms and the strategic allocation of surplus power for artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) clusters, represents a comprehensive effort to capitalize on the global "compute race."

## 1. Institutional Governance and the Facilitation of Export Growth

The Pakistan Software Export Board (PSEB) stands as the central pillar of the nation’s IT governance. Established in 1995 as a government-owned, guarantee-limited company under the Ministry of IT & Telecommunication, PSEB’s mission is to strengthen Pakistan’s IT industry and position it as a preferred outsourcing destination.1 Under the leadership of CEO Faisal Jeddy and the Federal Minister of IT, Ms. Shaza Fatima Khawaja, the board has transitioned from a simple registration body to a comprehensive facilitator of infrastructure, human capital, and global trade.1

The board operates from its headquarters in the Blue Area of Islamabad, overseeing an ecosystem that includes 43 Software Technology Parks (STPs) nationwide, encompassing over 1.9 million square feet of workspace and supporting 18,000 professionals.1 These STPs are not merely physical assets; they are strategic zones contributing over $100 million annually to IT exports and providing a domestic revenue contribution of $15 million.1 The registration process through the TechDestination portal has been optimized for the digital age, with SECP-registered companies achieving membership in approximately nine minutes.1

The benefits of PSEB registration extend beyond legal recognition. Registered entities gain access to IP whitelisting for banking, specialized tax facilitation desks, and entry into the Global Advisory & Innovation Network (GAIN).1 This institutional support is critical as the industry targets rapid export growth. In the first half of the 2024-25 fiscal year, ICT exports reached $1.86 billion, reflecting a 28% growth rate, while the total IT export figure for 2024 reached approximately $3 billion.1

Parallel to PSEB, the Special Technology Zones Authority (STZA) provides a high-incentive framework for large-scale technology investments.1 STZA licenses both Zone Developers and Zone Enterprises, offering ten-year tax holidays and duty-free imports of technical equipment.1 A notable example is the NASTP-ALPHA zone in Rawalpindi, which focuses on aerospace, AI, and cybersecurity.1 However, a significant regulatory development in early 2026 is the pause on new STZ notifications, a direct consequence of commitments made under the IMF’s Extended Fund Facility.1 This reflects a complex balancing act between sovereign industrial policy and global financial conditionalities.

## 2. Telecommunications Regulation and the Spectrum Landscape

The Pakistan Telecommunication Authority (PTA), established under the 1996 Re-organization Act, serves as the technical and operational regulator of the sector.1 Beyond its core functions of spectrum management and consumer protection, the PTA is currently overseeing the transition to 5G, a move essential for the low-latency requirements of modern AI and ML operations.1

The PTA’s licensing regime is exhaustive, managing diverse categories from Cellular Mobile Operators (CMOs) to Low-Power Wide-Area Networks (LPWAN) for IoT.1 For companies operating in the AI infrastructure space, the Class Value Added Services (CVAS) and Data CVAS licenses are mandatory for offering hosting, VPS, and networking services.1 Furthermore, the PTA enforces the Critical Telecom Data Infrastructure Security Regulations (CTDISR), which mandate rigorous cybersecurity audits and compliance with a national cybersecurity framework.1

The 5G spectrum auction, targeted for completion by February 2026, is a watershed moment for the industry.2 The federal government has tasked the PTA with introducing an additional 600 MHz of spectrum to alleviate the current shortage.2 Currently, Pakistani operators utilize only 274 MHz, leading to significant quality-of-service issues.2 PTA sources indicate that 80% of existing mobile devices are ready for mid-band frequencies (2300 and 2600 MHz).5 The 5G rollout is mandated to offer a minimum downlink speed of 50 Mbps, which will drastically improve the viability of mobile-based AI applications and distributed edge computing.5

## 3. Data Center Market Dynamics: From Karachi to Islamabad

Pakistan’s data center market is characterized by a high degree of centralization but is experiencing a rapid geographic shift. As of 2025, the IT load capacity stands at 23.53 MW, with a projected CAGR of 17.77%, reaching 53.30 MW by 2030.1 Karachi remains the dominant hub, holding 49% of the market share due to its proximity to international submarine cable landings.1 However, Islamabad and Rawalpindi are the fastest-growing hotspots, with an 18.70% CAGR.1 This shift is driven by the region’s cooler climate, which reduces chiller loads by approximately 7% relative to Karachi, and its proximity to the CPEC terrestrial fiber corridors.1

The market is maturing from Tier III standards toward Tier IV resilience, with Tier IV builds advancing at a CAGR of 16.40%.1 Major players like PTCL, Multinet, and Cybernet (RapidCompute) are being joined by new entrants leveraging unique assets. Mari Petroleum, for example, is developing data centers that utilize its internal gas and power assets, providing a hedge against grid volatility.1 Jazz has recently invested $8 million in a Tier III Digital Park to support fintech and cloud services.1 Furthermore, the emergence of AI-specific facilities, such as the Data Vault and Telenor Pakistan venture in Karachi, which houses over 3,000 NVIDIA H100 and H200 GPUs, signals the beginning of a specialized compute market.7

## 4. The Power-Compute Nexus: Surplus Energy as a Strategic Asset

A unique competitive advantage for Pakistan’s AI infrastructure is the national strategy to monetize surplus electricity. The energy sector currently grapples with a paradox of high tariffs and underutilized capacity, with coal-fired plants often operating at only 15% of their total potential.8 To address this, the Ministry of Finance and the Pakistan Crypto Council (PCC) have announced the allocation of 2,000 MW of surplus electricity specifically for Bitcoin mining and AI data centers.8

This initiative, led by Bilal Bin Saqib (CEO of the PCC and Special Assistant to the Prime Minister), aims to convert a fiscal liability—capacity charges—into a high-value digital asset.8 The power minister noted that the country has a 7,000 MW surplus, which can be provided at a marginal cost of approximately Rs 24 per kWh ($0.086/kWh).8 There are proposals to set up a special purpose vehicle (SPV) to subsidize this rate down to 4 cents per unit in exchange for profit-sharing in the compute business.8

The potential economic impact is staggering. It is estimated that 2,000 MW of power could generate up to 17,000 BTC annually, equivalent to $1.8 billion at current prices.9 Furthermore, by establishing a "Strategic Bitcoin Reserve," the government aims to hedge against economic instability and build a sovereign digital wealth fund.9 However, this plan has encountered friction with the IMF, which has raised objections over subsidized tariffs and grid stability.9 The resolution of these objections will be a critical determinant for the feasibility of large-scale AI clusters in Islamabad.

## 5. Connectivity and Latency: The Digital Silk Road

The infrastructure supporting AI/ML is as much about data transit as it is about compute. Pakistan’s international connectivity is undergoing a diversification from traditional submarine paths to terrestrial corridors. The China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC) terrestrial fiber optic project, stretching 820 km from Rawalpindi to the Khunjerab Pass, provides a direct land link to China.13 This $44 million project, implemented by Huawei and the Special Communication Organization (SCO), offers a low-latency alternative to undersea cables, which are often subject to disruptions and congestion.15

| Terrestrial (CPEC) | Rawalpindi to Khunjerab (China) | Low (Direct Land) | High (Secure Path) |
| Submarine (PEACE) | Karachi to East Africa / Europe | Ultra-Low (High Bandwidth) | Vulnerable to Sea Breaks |
| Submarine (SMW-6) | Karachi to Middle East / Europe | High Bandwidth (200+ Tbps) | Strategic Multi-Node |
| Terrestrial (Transit) | Central Asia / Iran | Moderate | Emerging Corridor |

The CPEC fiber link is capable of supporting 4 Tbps of data and serves as a backbone for the "Digital Silk Road".17 By connecting to the Pakistan East Africa Connecting Europe (PEACE) submarine cable in Karachi, this route creates a multi-dimensional digital highway linking Asia with Africa and Europe.18 For AI data centers in Islamabad, this provides a unique dual-homing advantage: low-latency access to Chinese AI ecosystems and high-bandwidth connectivity to Western markets.10

## 6. The Global AI Infrastructure Market: Hardware and GPU Clouds

Pakistan’s domestic build-out is taking place against a backdrop of a global AI infrastructure market valued at $135.81 billion in 2024, with a projected reach of $394.46 billion by 2030.1 The market is dominated by NVIDIA’s GPU compute architecture, particularly the H100 and the newly introduced Blackwell (B200/GB200) series.1

The procurement of such hardware in Pakistan is complicated by geopolitical trade policies and export controls. While the US government has allowed some exports of chips like the H200 to approved international customers, these are often subject to 25% taxes and rigorous national security screenings.22 These "roundabout tariffs" and logistics hurdles can increase the total cost of ownership (TCO) for local infrastructure providers.

A single NVIDIA H100 purchase costs approximately $25,000 to $30,000.27 Factoring in infrastructure costs, the break-even timeline against a $2.99/hour cloud rate is roughly 14 months of 24/7 usage.27 For research and development, cloud platforms like Lambda or RunPod are more economical, while for massive, continuous training, the 2,000 MW power reservation makes on-premises clusters in Pakistan highly competitive.

## 7. Fiscal Policy and the Finance Act 2025-26

The financial environment for AI and IT infrastructure is shaped by the Finance Act 2025-26 and subsequent FBR circulars. A primary focus of the new budget is the formalization of e-commerce and the digital economy. Section 6A of the Income Tax Ordinance now stipulates that all payments for digitally ordered goods or services are subject to tax.28 Payment intermediaries (banks and financial institutions) must collect 1% withholding tax on digital payments, while courier services must collect 2% on cash-on-delivery transactions.28

However, the IT and ITeS export sector remains a priority area for incentives. While general withholding tax rates for services were increased (from 4% to 8% for non-resident and 6% for resident entities), IT and IT-enabled services are specifically excluded from these increases.28 They continue to benefit from a lower 4% rate.28 Additionally, income tax exemptions for entities in erstwhile tribal areas have been extended until June 30, 2026.28

The National Tariff Policy 2025-30 further supports the sector by reducing customs duties on capital goods and raw materials.31 The zero-percent (0%) tariff slab has been extended to an additional 916 PCT codes, particularly impacting machinery and electrical equipment (HS 84 and 85).32 This reduction in import duties on server components and networking gear is essential for lowering the upfront capital expenditure (CAPEX) of data center builds.

## 8. Cybersecurity Compliance and Data Sovereignty

As Pakistan moves toward a compute-heavy model, the regulatory burden for cybersecurity has increased. The PTA mandates that all telecom operators and significant data processors undergo audits by registered cybersecurity firms.3 These audits must align with the Critical Telecom Data Infrastructure Security Regulations (CTDISR) and the National Cybersecurity Framework.1

In 2026, the industry has shifted toward "continuous compliance," moving away from point-in-time annual audits.34 Regulators now expect real-time monitoring, centralized logging, and ongoing vulnerability management.34 For entities processing personal information for over 250,000 residents, rigorous audits are mandatory, with executive certifications due by April of each year.35

The establishment of local AI data centers addresses concerns over data sovereignty. By processing data domestically, organizations can comply with national security policies and reduce the risk of foreign data interception.10 This is particularly critical for government agencies, which currently utilize the National Data Center for SaaS, PaaS, and IaaS requirements.1

## 9. Domain Administration and the PKNIC Framework

A vital but often overlooked component of the digital infrastructure is the management of the.PK domain namespace. Managed by the Pakistan Network Information Centre (PKNIC) since 1992,.PK domains are the digital identities of the nation.1 As of October 2025, domain pricing for Pakistan-based registrants stands at PKR 1,800 per year, while international registrants pay USD 15.99.37

PKNIC operates a Shared Registry System (SRS) Partner Program, allowing authorized channel partners to sell domains and hosting services.1 For a company seeking to provide integrated AI and web services, becoming an SRS partner is a logical step. This requires the generation of a 2048-bit CSR (Certificate Signing Request) and compliance with EPP (Extensible Provisioning Protocol) rules for automated interaction with the registry.39

The registration of.edu.pk domains remains a moderated process, requiring letters of authorization from educational institutes and verification of registration with government bodies.41 This ensures that the digital infrastructure supporting academia—critical for AI research—remains bona fide and secure.

## 10. The Talent Gap and Human Capital Development

The bottleneck for Pakistan’s AI ambitions remains human capital. While the country produces 75,000 IT graduates annually, industry demand is estimated at 100,000.1 More critically, only 42% of data science personnel possess the "expert credentials" required for high-end AI research and data center management.1 Uptime-accredited engineers command a 25% wage premium, reflecting the acute shortage of specialized infrastructure talent.1

To address this, the government has reintroduced tax rebates for full-time teachers and researchers.28 The Finance Act 2025 restored a 25% tax rebate for these individuals, though the amendment specifies that it will only be available until the 2025 tax year.28 Programs like e-Rozgaar and various provincial initiatives are aimed at upskilling the 300,000+ freelancers in Pakistan to move up the value chain toward AI-enabled services.1

## 11. Strategic Synthesis and Conclusion

The convergence of 5G rollout, the 2,000 MW power allocation for compute, and the CPEC terrestrial fiber corridors creates a uniquely favorable environment for an Islamabad-based AI/ML infrastructure firm. The strategic "pivot to compute" is not merely an IT initiative but a sovereign energy arbitrage strategy. By converting underutilized power into digital assets, Pakistan can bypass traditional manufacturing bottlenecks and participate directly in the global AI economy.

The key to success in this environment will be navigating the regulatory complexities of PTA and PSEB, securing favorable power tariffs via the Pakistan Crypto Council, and leveraging the latency advantages of the CPEC fiber link. As global demand for AI training and inference continues to outpace supply, Pakistan’s combination of low-cost energy, strategic geography, and a youthful, tech-engaged population positions it as a formidable new frontier in the architecture of global intelligence.

The future of Pakistan’s digital landscape depends on the successful execution of the 5G auction and the resolution of IMF-led fiscal tensions surrounding power subsidies. If these challenges are met, the country is poised to transition from an IT service provider to a global hub for the "Digital Silk Road," where data and intelligence flow as freely as the electricity that powers them.
